Ready-to-Teach 5E Lesson Plans

Full inquiry-based lesson plans in the Engage → Explore → Explain → Elaborate → Evaluate format. Each pairs with an episode, an experiment from the lab shelf, and a printable. (*Disclaimer: the Frizz would never plan this much. That's your job. She'd just drive.)

Big Idea: The digestive system is a one-way processing plant that turns food into usable energy and building materials.

Objectives

  • Sequence the organs of the digestive system in order.
  • Distinguish mechanical from chemical digestion.
  • Model how nutrients move from food into the bloodstream.

Materials

  • Episode: "For Lunch" (S1E2)
  • Crackers, zip bags, water, orange juice, paper towels (per pair)
  • Body-outline poster, organ cards, yarn "esophagus"
  • Printable: Get Messy Lab Report

Vocabulary

esophagusstomachsmall intestineenzymenutrientperistalsis

The 5 Es

Engage (10 min): Eat a cracker slowly. Ask: "It started salty… why is it sweet now?" Collect theories on the Wonder Wall.
Explore (25 min): Run the Cracker Digestion Demo (see Experiments). Students record each "organ stage" on the lab report.
Explain (20 min): Watch "For Lunch." Pause at each organ; students move organ cards onto the body outline and narrate what happens there.
Elaborate (20 min): Teams build a working "digestion conveyor" from tubes and funnels, then pitch it as a ride at "Digestionland."
Evaluate (15 min): Exit ticket: "Trace a bite of pizza from mouth to bloodstream in 5 steps." Rubric in Assessment section below.

Assessment

  • Exit ticket scored 0–2 per step (10 pts).
  • Lab report observations for accuracy of mechanical vs. chemical labeling.

Home Connection

Students interview a family member: "Where do YOU think food goes?" and diagram the answer together.

Standards alignment: NGSS 4-LS1-1 (structure & function); CCSS.ELA RI.4.3 (sequencing). Adapt to your state framework as needed.

Big Idea: Water moves endlessly between Earth's surface and sky, driven by energy from the Sun.

Objectives

  • Define evaporation, condensation, precipitation, and collection.
  • Model the water cycle and explain the Sun's role.
  • Track a "personal raindrop" through a narrative journey.

Materials

  • Episode: "Wet All Over" (S2E6)
  • Zip bags, blue food coloring, tape, sunny window
  • Ice cubes, metal tray, kettle or warm water (teacher demo)
  • Printable: Bus Log observation journal

Vocabulary

evaporationcondensationprecipitationcollectionwater vapor

The 5 Es

Engage: Hold a cold tray over warm water — droplets appear "from nowhere." Where did they come from?
Explore: Build Water Cycle in a Bag (see Experiments). Hang on the window; observe daily for a week in the Bus Log.
Explain: Watch "Wet All Over." Students label the four stages directly on their bags with dry-erase marker.
Elaborate: Write "The Autobiography of Drippy," a first-person raindrop story that must use all four vocabulary words correctly.
Evaluate: Label a blank water-cycle diagram; quiz-builder topic "Weather & Water."

Assessment

  • Diagram labeling (8 pts).
  • Raindrop narrative rubric: vocabulary accuracy + cause/effect clarity.

Home Connection

Family challenge: find three places water collects around your home after rain.

Standards alignment: NGSS 5-ESS2-1, MS-ESS2-4; CCSS.ELA W.3.3 (narrative).

Big Idea: Earth's surface changes both slowly (erosion, deposition) and quickly (eruptions) — and the same forces build up and wear away land.

Objectives

  • Explain how volcanic eruptions create new land.
  • Model an eruption and map where "lava" flows.
  • Compare fast and slow changes to Earth's surface.

Materials

  • Episodes: "Blows Its Top" (S2E1) and "Rocks and Rolls" (S3E12)
  • Volcano Island kit (see Experiments), trays
  • World map or volcano-location cards
  • Printable: KWL Chart

Vocabulary

magmalavacrusteruptionerosiondeposition

The 5 Es

Engage: Show a photo of a brand-new volcanic island. "This island is younger than you. How?"
Explore: Volcano Island eruptions in teams; students sketch lava flow paths and compare two eruptions.
Explain: Watch "Blows Its Top." Build the class consensus model: pressure → eruption → new rock → erosion begins.
Elaborate: Debate: "Volcanoes: builders or destroyers?" Teams must argue BOTH sides with evidence.
Evaluate: KWL final column + quick-write: "One fast change and one slow change to Earth's surface, with examples."

Assessment

  • Quick-write rubric (claim, evidence, vocabulary).
  • Team debate notes for evidence use.

Home Connection

Students hunt for erosion evidence (cracked sidewalks, muddy gutters) and photograph or sketch one example.

Standards alignment: NGSS 4-ESS1-1, 4-ESS2-1, MS-ESS2-2.

Big Idea: Plants and pollinators need each other — flowers make food for insects, and insects help flowers make seeds.

Objectives

  • Identify flower parts: petal, stem, leaf, root.
  • Describe how pollen moves between flowers.
  • Model pollination with a hands-on simulation.

Materials

  • Episode: "Goes to Seed" (S1E11)
  • Cheetos or cheese powder (the "pollen"), paper flowers, pipe-cleaner "bees"
  • Real flowers or magnified photos, magnifying glasses
  • Printable: Vocabulary Window

Vocabulary

pollenpollinateseednectarflower

The 5 Es

Engage: Pass around cheese-dust "pollen" — touch one paper flower, then another. Look at the dust trail you left!
Explore: Bee-hand simulation: students "buzz" between flowers and track pollen transfer with tally marks.
Explain: Watch "Goes to Seed." Examine real flowers with magnifiers and match parts to a labeled diagram.
Elaborate: Design a "dream flower" that would attract a specific pollinator; defend the design choices.
Evaluate: Draw-and-label: flower with arrows showing where pollen travels.

Assessment

  • Labeled drawing (5 pts).
  • Observation of simulation participation and tally accuracy.

Home Connection

Send home a "pollinator spotter" mini-book: count bees, butterflies, or hummingbirds seen in a week.

Standards alignment: NGSS 2-LS2-2, K-LS1-1.

Big Idea: Electricity needs a complete loop. Conductors, insulators, and switches control whether energy flows.

Objectives

  • Build a complete circuit that lights a bulb.
  • Classify materials as conductors or insulators.
  • Draw a circuit using standard symbols.

Materials

  • Episode: "Gets Charged" (S4E10)
  • D-cell batteries, mini bulbs or LEDs, insulated wire, tape, switches (or paperclip+brad switches)
  • Test items: keys, erasers, foil, pencils, coins, plastic spoons
  • Printable: Get Messy Lab Report

Vocabulary

circuitconductorinsulatorswitchcurrentbattery

The 5 Es

Engage: Hand each team a battery, bulb, and ONE wire. No instructions. "Make it light." (They will. Eventually. Gloriously.)
Explore: Free circuit building; then a conductor/insulator testing station with a class results chart.
Explain: Watch "Gets Charged." Introduce circuit symbols; students redraw their working circuit as a diagram.
Elaborate: Engineering challenge: build a "quiz buzzer" — a circuit with a switch that lights only when both teammates press.
Evaluate: Diagram check + quiz-builder topic "Energy & Electricity."

Assessment

  • Circuit diagram accuracy (symbols, closed loop).
  • Buzzer challenge: works + explained in writing.

Home Connection

Home energy audit: count the switches in one room and trace what each one controls.

Standards alignment: NGSS 4-PS3-2, 3-5-ETS1-1.

Big Idea: Whether something floats depends on its density — how much stuff is packed into its space — not just its weight.

Objectives

  • Predict and test which objects sink or float.
  • Explain floating using density and displaced water.
  • Engineer a foil boat that carries maximum cargo.

Materials

  • Episode: "Ups and Downs" (S2E13)
  • Tubs of water, test objects (marble, cork, apple, can of diet vs. regular soda), aluminum foil sheets, pennies as cargo
  • Printable: 3-2-1 Exit Ticket

Vocabulary

buoyancydensityfloatsinkdisplace

The 5 Es

Engage: Drop a tiny pebble (sinks) and show a photo of a giant steel ship (floats). "Explain THAT."
Explore: Sink/float prediction station; the diet-vs-regular soda can test blows minds every year.
Explain: Watch "Ups and Downs." Introduce density with the "same box, different stuff inside" analogy.
Elaborate: Regatta day! Each team gets one foil sheet: build a boat, load pennies until it sinks. Record and graph results.
Evaluate: 3-2-1 Exit Ticket + written answer: "Why did the winning boat hold more?"

Assessment

  • Exit ticket + explanation scored on use of "density" and "displace."
  • Regatta data table completeness.

Home Connection

Bathtub lab (with permission): find three surprising floaters or sinkers at home.

Standards alignment: NGSS 3-PS2-1, 5-PS1-1, 3-5-ETS1-2.

Teaching Philosophy

The Frizzle Method

Six field-tested principles behind the magic — each with a concrete move you can use tomorrow, no shrinking required.

"Take chances. Make mistakes. Get messy."

It's not a catchphrase. It's a scope and sequence.

🎲

1 · Take Chances

Say yes to the weird question. Student curiosity is a compass — follow it off the map sometimes.

Try it: Keep a "Wonder Wall" parking lot. Chase ONE student question per week into a 15-minute mini-investigation.
💥

2 · Make Mistakes

Wrong answers are data. A class that hides mistakes can't do science; a class that examines them can.

Try it: Start a "Favorite Mistake" board. Grade revisions and reflections, not just first attempts.
🖐️

3 · Get Messy

Hands first, worksheet second. Understanding sticks to fingers that have touched the phenomenon.

Try it: Every unit gets one build-day or demo-day BEFORE any notes. Prep a "mess kit" (wipes, trays, aprons) so mess is cheap.
💺

4 · Seatbelts, Everyone

Adventure needs structure. Routines and safety rituals are what make bold lessons possible.

Try it: Assign rotating job badges (Materials Manager, Safety Officer, Timekeeper) so chaos has a chassis.
❓

5 · Ask Questions First

Answers are the caboose, not the engine. Start with a phenomenon and let the questions pile up.

Try it: Open each unit with one strange demo + 10 silent minutes of "I notice / I wonder" before explaining anything.
🕸️

6 · Look for Connections

Everything connects to everything — science to stories, math, art, and recess.

Try it: End units with a giant concept web on butcher paper connecting the science to two other subjects.
